# 0.2 How the handbook works

## Summary

The handbook explains durable concepts and guidance; live records hold current responsibilities and work. Numbered sections help you locate a topic, and status labels show whether material is adopted, proposed, or still being drafted. Editing or publishing a page does not adopt a policy.

## 0.2.1 Chapters, sections, subsections, and appendices {#chapters-sections-and-appendices}

The numbered handbook has four chapters: **0 Introduction**, **1 DNA**, **2 Leadership**, and **3 Staff**. Appendices A–D hold reference materials, further reading, citations, and the glossary. These are the planned homes; existing pages remain available while sections are added.

Numbers show where a topic sits. **0** identifies this chapter; **0.2** identifies this section; **0.2.1** identifies this subsection. Appendix numbering follows the same pattern, such as **A.1.2**.

A chapter landing page summarizes the chapter and links to its sections. A section usually has its own page, with named subsections inside it. Each new page has a Summary, a status, and a last-updated date. Summary and source lists are page elements, not extra numbered sections.

## 0.2.2 Beliefs, current practice, proposals, and open questions {#reading-status}

**Beliefs explain our outlook.** They describe the reasoning and values behind our work. A belief alone does not establish a process, assign authority, or approve a new requirement.

**Status shows adoption.** An adopted page describes established guidance. Draft pages are unfinished material for review; proposal pages describe possible future practice; experimental pages describe trials; reference pages provide supporting material. Read the page's status and any qualifications before treating an instruction as settled.

**Labels show unresolved decisions.** A **Proposal** block describes a possible practice. A **To clarify** block identifies a question still needing an answer. A draft remains a draft even where no individual block appears. Rewriting, numbering, or publishing it does not change decision rights or adopt its recommendations.

## 0.2.3 Where authoritative information lives {#where-authoritative-information-lives}

The existing contributor guidance separates information this way:

| Information | Where to look |
| --- | --- |
| Durable definitions, expectations, and repeatable guides | The handbook, checking page status |
| Current roles, owners, governance, priorities, and work | [Atlas](https://sapiensfirst.org/atlas) or live records linked from it |
| Current policy recommendations and campaign materials | The relevant [Sapiens First website](https://sapiensfirst.org) pages, including [campaigns](https://sapiensfirst.org/campaigns) |

For example, the handbook can explain a role; Atlas records who holds it now. An empty or unclear record does not imply that a decision has been made. Ask the relevant role holder when a current assignment or record is unclear.

::: clarify
These information boundaries reflect existing handbook and contributor guidance. Organizational confirmation of the boundaries, and responsibility for resolving conflicting records, remain open. Do not infer new authority from this draft.
:::

## 0.2.4 How to suggest changes {#how-to-suggest-changes}

If wording is confusing or information seems outdated, tell the person responsible for that area or suggest an edit. Identify the page, explain the problem, and include a source or proposed wording when you can. Repository contributors can follow the contributor guide's editing instructions.

Keep a factual correction or editorial change distinct from a proposed organizational decision. When suggesting a new rule, show what needs agreement and preserve its proposal status until the appropriate decision has been made.

::: clarify
Who reviews handbook changes, who approves policy changes, and how those decisions are recorded have not been specified. This page establishes no new reviewer, approval process, or decision authority.
:::
