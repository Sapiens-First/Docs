# Metrics and learning

A metric helps us notice whether something is changing. It is useful when it helps us make a better decision.

Start with the outcome you care about. Then ask what you can observe, how you'll collect it, and what you would do differently after seeing the result.

## Measure the result you mean

Counting activities can tell us how much work happened. Understanding outcomes tells us whether it helped.

For example, the number of workshops delivered measures activity. Whether participants go on to organize a meeting tells us something about the workshop's effect. Neither number tells the whole story; conversations and observations can help explain it.

::: info Proposal: where metrics belong
The working notes call for metrics attached to objectives or domains, with a responsible role or circle. One proposed approach is to link each metric to the outcome or product/service it measures, then link accountability to the relevant role or circle.

This keeps the meaning of the measure separate from the person currently responsible for it. The exact relationship to objectives, products, programs, and circles is still to be decided. Atlas does not yet establish this metric schema.
:::

## Proposed metric definition

| Field | What it explains |
| --- | --- |
| Name and purpose | What we measure and what decision it informs |
| Related work or objective | Which result it helps us understand |
| Definition | The unit, population, period, and calculation |
| Data source | Where the observations come from |
| Responsible role or circle | Who maintains the record and reviews the result |
| Review rhythm | When it is useful to look at it |
| Baseline and target, if appropriate | Where we started and what we're aiming for |
| Limits | What the metric misses or could misrepresent |

Specific definitions, targets, and results belong in Atlas or the live records linked from it. The handbook holds the agreed schema and review practice once adopted.

## Suggested review practice

1. **Define.** Agree on the outcome and the meaning of the measure.
2. **Monitor.** Gather observations consistently.
3. **Analyze.** Look for patterns and possible explanations.
4. **Reflect.** Decide what to keep, change, or test next.

A number moving in the right direction doesn't automatically prove that our work caused the change. Check the context and the quality of the data.

::: details Proposal: models of movement growth

The working notes suggest modeling how recruitment, coaching capacity, active organizers, and chapters affect one another. A model could help us compare possible investments and identify constraints.

For example, more recruits may not lead to more active organizers if nobody has time to welcome and support them. More trained leaders may not produce more chapters without members and ongoing support.

Define each variable, the time period, the assumptions, and the evidence behind it. Compare the model's predictions with actual results and revise it. The notes' “R0” idea is an exploratory analogy, not an established metric or a guarantee of exponential growth.
:::
